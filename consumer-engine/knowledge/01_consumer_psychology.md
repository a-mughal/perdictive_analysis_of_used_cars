# 01 — How a human decides to buy

A working distillation of the consumer-behaviour literature, organised as *tags* the agents must
cite. Each tag names its source. Agents reason **from** these, not around them.

---

## A. Two systems, one wallet — Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

`kahneman.system1` — Almost every Amazon purchase under ~$30 is a System-1 decision: fast,
associative, image-driven, resolved in seconds. The buyer does not read; they recognise.
Implication: the main image and the first three words of the title do 80% of the work.

`kahneman.system2_trigger` — System 2 (slow, deliberate) switches on only when something feels
*wrong*: price above the category anchor, a conflicting review, an unfamiliar brand on a safety
item. A listing that raises no alarm is never scrutinised. A listing that raises one alarm is
scrutinised for everything.

`kahneman.loss_aversion` — Losses weigh roughly twice gains. "Won't damage your desk" outsells
"holds strong". "Never fish a cable off the floor again" outsells "keeps cables tidy". Frame the
avoided loss, not the gained benefit.

`kahneman.anchoring` — The first price seen sets the reference. In a search grid, the anchor is the
median of the first row, not the cheapest item. A product 1.5–2× the anchor needs a *visible*
reason in the thumbnail; 3×+ needs a different search term.

`kahneman.availability` — Vivid, recent, easily recalled cases dominate judgement. One vivid 1-star
photo of a clip that ripped paint off a wall outweighs 500 silent 5-stars. Reviews with photos
are weighted ~5–10× by shoppers.

`kahneman.wysiati` — "What you see is all there is." Buyers do not imagine missing information;
they assume it doesn't exist. If the listing does not show the product on a thick cable, the buyer
concludes it does not fit thick cables.

`kahneman.peak_end` — Memory of an experience is set by its peak moment and its end. For a physical
product: the unboxing peak, and the moment it fails (or is removed). Design the removal experience
as carefully as the install.

## B. Predictable irrationality — Ariely, *Predictably Irrational*

`ariely.relativity` — People cannot value in absolute terms; they compare. A decoy (a deliberately
worse option at similar price) makes the target look excellent. In a search grid, position your
listing so the *adjacent* results are the decoys — i.e., pick a title that lands you next to the
bulk packs, not next to the premium brands.

`ariely.zero_price` — "Free" is not a price point, it's an emotion. Free included accessories
(prep wipe, spare heads) are valued far above their cost.

`ariely.endowment` — Once a buyer imagines owning it, they value it more. Lifestyle images that
put the product in a room *like theirs* trigger imagined ownership. Product-on-white does not.

`ariely.pain_of_paying` — Bundling reduces the salience of each cost; itemising increases it. One
kit price beats "base $X, heads $Y".

## C. The persuasion levers — Cialdini, *Influence*

`cialdini.social_proof` — Rating count matters more than rating average once both are above ~4.3
and ~500. A new listing's job is to reach 100 reviews; nothing else matters until then.

`cialdini.authority` — Named components ("3M VHB", "Nylon 66") borrow authority. Vague claims
("premium adhesive") lend none and invite suspicion.

`cialdini.scarcity` — Works weakly on commodities and *backfires* on Amazon ("Only 3 left" on a
$8 clip reads as a small, unreliable seller).

`cialdini.consistency` — A buyer who has already committed to a category term ("magnetic") will
not switch to "adhesive" mid-search. Match the term they arrived with.

`cialdini.reciprocity` — An honest "this will NOT work on textured walls" in the bullets builds
more trust than five positive bullets. The concession is repaid with belief.

## D. The physical act of shopping — Underhill, *Why We Buy*

`underhill.conversion_by_touch` — In stores, touch converts. Online, the substitute is the
*hands-in-frame* image and the video. A hand holding the product tells the buyer its true size.

`underhill.decompression` — Shoppers need a moment to orient before they evaluate. Online this is
the first image: it must orient (what is it, how big, where does it go) before it persuades.

`underhill.the_butt_brush` — Friction kills. Every extra click, every "see size chart", every
variation dropdown loses a slice of buyers. One SKU, one obvious choice.

`underhill.interception_rate` — Sales are proportional to the share of shoppers who *engage*,
not who pass by. On Amazon, engagement = click-through rate from the grid. CTR is the KPI that
predicts everything downstream.

## E. Jobs to be done — Christensen, *Competing Against Luck*; Ulwick, *What Customers Want*

`jtbd.functional` — The job the product is hired for. "Keep this cable where I left it."
`jtbd.emotional` — How the buyer wants to feel. "I am a person with an organised desk."
`jtbd.social` — How they want to be seen. "My setup looks intentional on camera."
`jtbd.hiring_moment` — The trigger that makes them search *today*: a cable fell behind the desk
again; a new standing desk arrived; a work-from-home video call showed the mess.
`jtbd.firing` — What they used before and why it failed (tape, zip ties, a previous clip). The
fired product tells you the unmet need more accurately than any survey.
`ulwick.outcome_statement` — Phrase needs as: *minimise* the time it takes to [X] / *minimise* the
likelihood that [Y]. E.g. "minimise the time to swap a cable", "minimise the likelihood the
clip falls off within a year". Score each outcome on Importance × (1 − Satisfaction).
`ulwick.underserved` — Outcomes with high importance and low satisfaction are the only ones worth
innovating on. Over-served outcomes (already great) are where to *cut* cost.

## F. Kano model — Kano, 1984

`kano.must_be` — Expected; absence enrages, presence is unnoticed. (Adhesive holds; cable stays.)
`kano.performance` — More is better, linearly. (Weight capacity, pack count.)
`kano.delighter` — Unexpected; creates the 5-star review and the word-of-mouth. (A hinged lid on a
clip. A prep wipe in the box.) Delighters decay into must-bes within 2–3 years as competitors copy.
`kano.indifferent` — Nobody cares. Cut it. (Branded pouch for $8 clips.)
`kano.reverse` — Some buyers actively dislike it. (Glossy finish; loud packaging.)

## G. Choice architecture — Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge*; Schwartz, *Paradox of Choice*

`nudge.default` — Whatever is pre-selected is chosen ~70%+ of the time. On a variation listing,
the default variant carries the listing.
`schwartz.overload` — Beyond ~6 visible options, purchase probability *falls* and regret rises.
Do not offer 4 colours × 3 sizes. Offer 2 colours, one size that fits everything.
`nudge.friction_asymmetry` — Make the desired action (buy, re-order) frictionless and the undesired
one (return) fair but not frictionless.

## H. Diffusion & growth — Rogers; Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*; Sharp, *How Brands Grow*

`rogers.innovators` — First 2.5% buy *because* it's new. They tolerate flaws and write reviews.
`moore.chasm` — The early majority will not buy until they see people like them buying. On
Amazon the chasm is roughly the gap between 30 and 300 reviews.
`sharp.mental_availability` — Being *thought of* in the buying moment beats being preferred.
Own a search term, not a "brand". `sharp.physical_availability` — Being easy to buy (Prime,
in stock, first page) beats being better.
`sharp.double_jeopardy` — Small brands have fewer buyers *and* lower loyalty. Don't plan on
repeat purchase for a commodity; plan on being found again.

## I. Trust in an unfamiliar seller — Sutherland, *Alchemy*; Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*

`sutherland.costly_signal` — Effort the seller visibly wasted on the buyer's behalf (a heavy box,
a printed guide, a spare part) signals confidence in the product. Cheapness signals the opposite.
`sutherland.satisficing` — People do not maximise; they pick the first option that is *good enough
and unlikely to be a mistake*. Design to be the safe choice, not the best choice.
`dunford.competitive_alternative` — Positioning starts from "what would they use if we didn't
exist?" Not "other clips" — often it's *tape* or *doing nothing*. Beat that.
`dunford.best_fit_customer` — Name the buyer for whom this is obviously the right choice, and
accept losing the others. On Amazon, that buyer is defined by a search term.

## J. Post-purchase — the part sellers forget

`post.cognitive_dissonance` — After paying, buyers look for reasons they were right. Give them one
inside the box (a card that says "you chose the one with the lid" works).
`post.review_trigger` — Reviews are written at emotional peaks: delight at unboxing or rage at
failure. Neutral products get neither. Engineer one small delight into the first 60 seconds.
`post.return_reason` — Amazon's top return reasons for accessories: "didn't fit", "not as
described", "arrived damaged". Each maps to a listing fix, not a product fix.

## How the buyer agent must use this file

For every step of the decision trace, cite ≥1 tag. For every objection, name the framework that
predicts it. For every must-have, classify it (`kano.*`). Compute `ulwick.outcome_statement`
scores for the top 8 outcomes. Untagged reasoning is worth half.
